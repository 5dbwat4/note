So the first part I want to talk about is the data. The data remains very important for all of these processes. And for RL, there's an additional wrinkle of curriculum, what difficulties of problems do you want to throw in? In SFT, you don't really think about difficulty very much because you just jam the data in there. For whatever data that you want, you just do pre-training losses and the model is forced to learn from these. In SFT, there's a little bit more of a subtlety. We talked quite a bit about the hallucination issues, and maybe sometimes you don't want to train on some of this data. But for RL, there's this additional wrinkle that if your problems are too hard, you get no rewards. And if you get no rewards, you have no signal. And if you have no signal, you can't learn. So it's very important to have broad coverage of the right kinds of examples. And so what the Kimi folks do is first of all, they try to get a whole big, broad range of coverage of data. Unsurprising, they exclude multiple choice because that's covered in lots of different domains. They want things that require long, deep thought. And their argument is maybe multiple choice doesn't have that. But maybe this last one I think is maybe the most important idea. This appears in a lot of different RL papers. You basically filter all the examples for things that either pass or sorry, you filter them based on a best of k filter. So here you take examples. And if they succeed on best of 8, if you sample eight times and the model can do it at least once, then that's not a great RL example, because it's already at the edge of the model's capabilities. You're not going to teach it anything particularly new. And so you only look at examples that basically fail this test, because that allows us to essentially pick the right kinds of problems. And so this saves compute. It allows you to skip problems entirely. You can also filter on both sides to get problems that are neither too hard nor too easy.
